# CSE Spack discussion notes supporting public research

8 October 2026. This note uses public, primary sources and the described CSE scenario. It does not assert the contents or authority of the unavailable organizational white paper. “Spack” is the software's name. The [CSE SOP](cse_software_stack_sop_v1.md) and [shared SOP](software_stack_sop_v1.md) are local draft procedures, not proof of an approved authorization package.

## The public operating context

HPCMP describes five supercomputing resource centers supporting demanding RDT&E and acquisition engineering computation. Its public center guidance says the computing environment follows a standard baseline but varies by vendor and architecture; applications, compilers, and tools are exposed through modules. The same guidance explicitly describes users bringing and installing their own code in their space, while assigning users responsibility for restrictions and malicious software and stating that running servers or software that communicates remotely requires prior authorization. These are public examples of the program's operating model; user-installed code is not a precedent that automatically authorizes a CSE stack published for many users or a new service. [HPCMP computation centers](https://www.hpc.mil/solution-areas/computation-centers); [MHPCC introductory guide](https://www.centers.hpc.mil/users/docs/mhpcc/introGuide.html); [HPC Centers getting started](https://www.centers.hpc.mil/users/index.html).

The guides distinguish classified and unclassified resources, restricted software access, and system-specific environments. Therefore, a shared CSE *process* can be proposed, but security boundary, transfer, approval, source-access, and binary compatibility decisions should be recorded for each applicable system/enclave. The public pages alone do not establish that binaries from a twin system are approved for transfer or compatible with its destination. [MHPCC introductory guide](https://www.centers.hpc.mil/users/docs/mhpcc/introGuide.html); [HPC Centers getting started](https://www.centers.hpc.mil/users/index.html).

NIST SP 800-223 explains why HPC security differs by system architecture, mission, and application. NIST SP 800-234 (final, May 2026) provides an HPC-specific overlay built on the **moderate** SP 800-53B baseline, with guidance on 60 tailored controls. It recognizes performance as a fundamental system requirement and separates Access, Management, Computing, and Data Storage Zones. Its treatment of user-developed software is particularly relevant: CM-7(5) says a per-program allowlist may be impractical where users can bring, develop, and compile code; CM-11 distinguishes limited-group, non-privileged user software from system-wide software available to all authorized users; SI-7's overlay guidance addresses system software rather than user-installed software. These points protect the user-code model but do not automatically exempt a CSE stack managed by staff and broadly published through modules. The approving authority should classify that publication under the site's selected controls; its unprivileged build account and path alone do not settle the issue. The overlay can be customized by operators; it is not a self-executing approval or a waiver of selected DoD controls. The publication also says NIST guidelines do not apply to national security systems without approval by the officials with policy authority. [NIST SP 800-223](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/223/final); [NIST SP 800-234, introduction, §§3.8–3.9, §4 and authority statement](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/SpecialPublications/NIST.SP.800-234.pdf).

## CSE adoption, mission benefit, and the whole operating outcome

NIST SP 800-223 §3.1 connects usable security mechanisms and tolerable performance effects with adoption in scientific HPC. Section 4.5 recommends benchmarking security-tool overhead and choosing appropriate controls for different node roles. This directly supports considering usability and performance in the security assessment. It does not establish that any particular compiler setting harms CSE or that fewer CSE users necessarily increases total risk. [NIST SP 800-223, printed pp. 10 and 17](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/SpecialPublications/NIST.SP.800-223.pdf).

**Application to the described CSE service:** common, optimized packages and familiar modules can reduce repeated setup and ease movement between machines. Their shared review, testing, maintenance, and release records reach users who adopt those packages. If added requirements reduce package availability, delay updates, or make builds unsuitable for workloads, independent user builds may increase. That can move more acquisition, configuration, patching, and support work into separate research teams. This is a reasoned risk scenario, not a measured CSE outcome. Shared distribution also makes protection of the common release important because one compromised release can reach many users.

The meeting can assess that scenario using the planned trials and existing support feedback: software setup and porting effort, reasons for independent builds, correctness and performance, review/update turnaround, and staff effort. Benefits to allocation use and reduced end-of-allocation pressure remain plausible but unquantified. No new telemetry system is needed before sharing the SOPs and agreeing on a joint trial. Continued manual delivery should be assessed against the coverage and update response a two-person team can sustain.

## What RMF supports and what it leaves to the organization

DoDI 8510.01 is the public DoD RMF issuance. It directs categorization and control selection under CNSSI 1253, implementation of NIST SP 800-53 controls, use of DoD-specific assignments and overlays, and continuous monitoring. NIST describes the RMF as a flexible process of categorizing, selecting, implementing, assessing, authorizing, and monitoring controls; a senior official makes the authorization decision. Thus user-space execution and no elevated privileges narrow some risks but do not, by themselves, remove a managed shared stack from the host system's security assessment. The actual authorization boundary and inherited/site controls need confirmation from the system owner and security authority. [DoDI 8510.01](https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/851001p.pdf); [NIST RMF overview](https://csrc.nist.gov/projects/risk-management/about-rmf); [NIST SP 800-37 Rev. 2](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/37/r2/final).

The current SP 800-53 control catalog is **Release 5.2.0** (August 2025); NIST's change list shows no change to CM-3 or CM-4 from the cited 5.1 text. **CM-3** calls for the organization to determine and document the types of changes that are configuration-controlled; review and approve or disapprove proposed controlled changes with security/privacy impact analysis; document decisions; implement approved changes; retain records for an organization-defined period; monitor change activity; and coordinate oversight through an organization-defined change element. **CM-4** calls for security/privacy impact analysis *before* implementing system changes. CM-3(2) addresses testing and documentation; the HPC overlay says Computing Zone testing should emphasize performance and recognizes that a full-scale test environment may be impractical. CM-3(3) discusses automated implementation using organization-defined mechanisms **if that enhancement is selected**. The control text itself does not specify a form named RFC, one RFC per package, Ansible, GitLab, a Spack server, or the described timeline. Those might still be valid local selections, so ask for the applicable control implementation or directive. [NIST SP 800-53 Release 5.2.0 notice and change list](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/53/r5/upd1/final); [NIST SP 800-53 CM-3 and CM-4 text](https://csrc.nist.gov/CSRC/media/Projects/risk-management/800-53%20Downloads/800-53r5/SP_800-53_v5_1-derived-OSCAL.pdf); [NIST SP 800-234, CM-3(2)](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/SpecialPublications/NIST.SP.800-234.pdf).

NIST SP 800-161 Rev. 1 describes supply-chain risk management as assessment and mitigation across the organization, product, and supplier lifecycle. It supports retaining source, recipe, build, approval, and distribution evidence. It does not prescribe a particular Spack deployment topology. [NIST SP 800-161 Rev. 1 update 1](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/161/r1/upd1/final).

**CUI does not by itself select SP 800-171 for a DoD-operated HPC system.** SP 800-171 Rev. 3 states that it recommends confidentiality requirements for CUI on *nonfederal* systems and organizations, for use in contracts or other agreements; DoDI 8582.01 similarly addresses non-DoD systems processing DoD CUI. The meeting should identify which entity operates each system and which authorization/contractual regime applies before citing 800-171 as its governing baseline. [NIST SP 800-171 Rev. 3](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/171/r3/final); [NIST's scope explanation](https://csrc.nist.gov/projects/protecting-controlled-unclassified-information/sp-800-171); [DoDI 8582.01](https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodi/858201p.pdf).

The DoD CIO's January 2022 open-source memorandum calls for considering existing government and open-source solutions and applying careful supply-chain risk management and cyber threat testing to externally maintained code. It places suitability and risk responsibility with program managers. It supports evaluating Spack and its components through a risk process; it neither rejects OSS because it is open source nor grants automatic approval to deploy it. [DoD CIO, *Software Development and Open Source Software*](https://dodcio.defense.gov/Portals/0/Documents/Library/SoftwareDev-OpenSource.pdf).

## Spack capabilities and their limits, using the project's Spack 1.2 target

| Concern | Documented capability | Control/decision still needed |
|---|---|---|
| Clean builds and source identity | Spack 1.2's documented defaults include `config:dirty: false` and `config:checksum: true`. The former cleans selected environment variables; the latter verifies downloaded source against recorded checksums. | Verify the *effective* configuration and source/recipe revisions for the CSE build. Neither setting proves that reviewed code is benign or that a binary was built by an authorized builder. [Spack 1.2 settings](https://spack.readthedocs.io/en/v1.2.0/config_yaml.html). |
| Package recipes | Spack supports organization repositories from local paths or Git, commit pinning, and repository ordering/overrides. | A private, access-controlled GitLab recipe repo is an available governance choice, especially for CSE overlays. Spack does not require every recipe to be copied into one private repo. Pin and record the built-in and overlay revisions and effective search order. [Spack 1.2 repositories](https://spack.readthedocs.io/en/v1.2.0/repositories.html). |
| Restricted destination and binary import | Spack build caches support cryptographic package signing and verification by default. `--use-buildcache only` prevents source-build fallback, which otherwise occurs by default. | Define who may sign, which public keys are trusted, what may cross the enclave boundary, compatibility/externals checks, and destination acceptance. A signature proves origin under the trusted key and integrity of signed content; it is not a vulnerability or malware clearance. [Spack 1.2 build caches](https://spack.readthedocs.io/en/v1.2.0/binary_caches.html). |
| Compiler flags | External compiler definitions accept `cflags`, `cxxflags`, `fflags`, `cppflags`, `ldflags`, and `ldlibs` applied to specs using that compiler; toolchains also provide scoped compiler constraints. | Treat each requested flag as a testable profile for a named compiler, linker, package, and target. Inspect actual compile/link commands and binaries; assess correctness and representative workload performance before a fleet default. Externals and reused binaries need separate evidence. The public Spack documentation does not establish a universal GCC/CCE/AOCC/Intel flag equivalence or a DoD-wide flag mandate. [Spack 1.2 package settings](https://spack.readthedocs.io/en/v1.2.0/packages_yaml.html); [Spack 1.2 toolchains](https://spack.readthedocs.io/en/v1.2.0/toolchains_yaml.html); [GCC code-generation options](https://gcc.gnu.org/onlinedocs/gcc/Code-Gen-Options.html); [GCC instrumentation options](https://gcc.gnu.org/onlinedocs/gcc/Instrumentation-Options.html). |

The local [compiler-hardening alignment research](hpcmp_compiler_hardening_policy_alignment_v1.md) has a more detailed flag-by-flag primary-source assessment. The local [supply-chain research](spack_supply_chain_security_primary_source_research_v1.md) covers source and binary admission in more depth. Their proposed controls remain proposals until adopted by the proper authority.

## Practical meeting position from these sources

### Package-repository release and maintenance check

The following was checked against the live public GitHub API on 8 October 2026, separately from Spack core's release numbering. The [published package releases](https://api.github.com/repos/spack/spack-packages/releases?per_page=100) are `v2025.07.0` (18 July 2025), `v2025.11.0` (15 November 2025), `v2026.03.0` (2 April 2026), and `v2026.06.0` (22 June 2026). The [tag inventory](https://api.github.com/repos/spack/spack-packages/tags?per_page=100) contains no numbered package-repository `.1` patch release at this observation. Spack core's `v1.2.1` and `v1.2.2` releases update the tool and do not establish a corresponding recipe-repository patch cadence. [Core releases](https://github.com/spack/spack/releases).

The [branch inventory](https://api.github.com/repos/spack/spack-packages/branches?per_page=100) includes `releases/v2025.07`, `releases/v2025.11`, `releases/v2026.03`, `releases/v2026.06`, and an as-yet untagged `releases/v2026.10`. A concrete maintenance example exists on `releases/v2025.07`: commit [`32c54f0906004d7fd1f72fd1b5970bf2bf094e26`](https://github.com/spack/spack-packages/commit/32c54f0906004d7fd1f72fd1b5970bf2bf094e26), dated 22 July 2025, fixes the GCC recipe's `microarchitecture_flags_from_target` handling. Its parent is the `.0` tag commit `4b8bb3514838ce9e8bbc2ca7f98e62dbd7100c63`; its changed file is `repos/spack_repo/builtin/packages/gcc/package.py`. The associated [upstream PR #671](https://github.com/spack/spack-packages/pull/671) was merged into `develop` on 21 July. This establishes a targeted recipe correction on the release branch after the tagged release without a new numbered package release. It establishes no general support duration or maintenance guarantee. [Commit metadata](https://api.github.com/repos/spack/spack-packages/commits/32c54f0906004d7fd1f72fd1b5970bf2bf094e26).

The [June package-release notes](https://github.com/spack/spack-packages/releases/tag/v2026.06.0) say the broad release brings new packages, package-version updates, and deprecations that are not backported to earlier releases. They include CVE-related deprecations, so continuing an older baseline entails reviewing relevant newer findings and selecting necessary fixes. The [March release notes](https://github.com/spack/spack-packages/releases/tag/v2026.03.0) explicitly describe an out-of-phase release relative to core. Neither the observed dates nor these notes establish a guaranteed quarterly schedule.

**Application to CSE's clarified intent:** use a mature package-repository release line, normally one broad release behind, with approximately 90 days as a maturation target for a broad baseline change. Permit reviewed fixes sooner, whether supplied as a release-branch commit or a versioned CSE overlay. Record exact commits and affected validation; do not automatically track a moving branch or require every fix to restart the baseline's observation period. Spack supports independent branch/tag/commit pins for recipe repositories. [Spack repository configuration](https://spack.readthedocs.io/en/v1.2.0/repositories.html).

This is clarified proposed wording for the meeting. The current CSE SOP §8.1 and the earlier snapshot research note instead describe a strict minimum of 90 elapsed days; that wording is narrower than the intent explained for this meeting. The [personal discussion notes](cse_spack_issm_meeting_brief_v1.md) capture the intended distinction for discussion before revising the SOP. This research does not change the actual trial pins or approve a baseline update.

### Discussion application

**Stage of work, as clarified by CSE:** the initial sixteen-package trial across four varied systems was discovery: establish whether Spack could build the packages and whether the builds were usable, document system differences and problems, and develop the SOP from that experience. It used baseline optimization without deliberate architecture-specific tuning and had no user-facing release. Further qualification, architecture tuning, and user evaluation belong to subsequent work. The intended learning cycle is build, document findings, update the SOP, run through it, and iterate with security participation. Public control guidance informs the applicable requirements; it does not establish that this exploratory trial completed production qualification.

1. Ask the ISSM to identify the applicable system/enclave, whether the CSE publication is treated as system-wide or user-managed software, the security outcome, controlling policy or selected RMF control, and expected evidence. Map the *draft* CSE procedure to that outcome and agree on the remaining gap. Record an organization-specific server, GitLab/Ansible workflow, private repo, flags, RFC form, or deadline as a local direction where its authority is identified. [DoDI 8510.01](https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/851001p.pdf); [NIST SP 800-234, CM-11/SI-7](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/SpecialPublications/NIST.SP.800-234.pdf); [NIST SP 800-53 CM-3/CM-4](https://csrc.nist.gov/CSRC/media/Projects/risk-management/800-53%20Downloads/800-53r5/SP_800-53_v5_1-derived-OSCAL.pdf).
2. Start with what the exploratory builds have taught the team. Review both SOPs with security, identify the requirements governing current discovery work, and exercise the applicable steps through further iterations. Agree on the additional evidence needed as the work advances toward qualification and publication. Architecture tuning and representative performance assessment remain later work; the initial baseline builds do not establish those results. NIST's HPC overlay supports zone-aware performance testing and acknowledges limited full-scale test capacity. The broader year-long plan and the white paper's shorter implementation schedule need concrete deliverables and dependencies before they can be compared. [NIST SP 800-234, CM-3(2)](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/SpecialPublications/NIST.SP.800-234.pdf); [NIST SP 800-53 CM-3/CM-4](https://csrc.nist.gov/CSRC/media/Projects/risk-management/800-53%20Downloads/800-53r5/SP_800-53_v5_1-derived-OSCAL.pdf).
3. Describe the proposed trust boundary: CSE builders use Spack in a controlled area; users would consume CSE-published software through modules; restricted destinations would consume signed, compatible binaries only after approved transfer and destination acceptance. Ask what a proposed “Spack server” actually serves, in which zone/enclave it runs, and which approval covers it. GitLab and Ansible can be considered for the authorized build/release workflow; their names do not substitute for evidence that it meets the selected control. [MHPCC introductory guide](https://www.centers.hpc.mil/users/docs/mhpcc/introGuide.html); [Spack 1.2 build caches](https://spack.readthedocs.io/en/v1.2.0/binary_caches.html); [NIST SP 800-234, zone model](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/SpecialPublications/NIST.SP.800-234.pdf).

## Source limitations

The organizational paper, RMF Knowledge Service assignments, system security plans, ATOs, local change procedure, enclave transfer rules, and compiler/STIG mappings were not available here. Public documents establish the governing framework and Spack mechanisms, not whether the ISSM's local proposal is binding or whether the current CSE draft satisfies every selected control. The DoDI PDF link is official, but direct fetching was intermittently denied by its host; the official search-index text and NIST publications supported the bounded summary above. No public source reviewed here establishes the specific Spack topology, approval form, cross-compiler flag set, or implementation deadline described in the meeting scenario; the relevant local authorities must be checked.
